Sleep Deprivation and Academic Performance Among High School Students: A Cross-Sectional Analysis of 412 Students

Students averaging fewer than six hours of sleep per school night reported significantly lower grades and higher rates of daytime fatigue, our largest survey to date finds.

By Ava Chen
Class of 2026 · Reviewed by the MRS Editorial Board
MRS Journal · Vol. 5, No. 2 · 2026
Cite as: Chen A. MRS J. 2026;5(2):14–22.

Abstract

Background: Adolescents require 8–10 hours of sleep per night, yet academic pressure and screen use have driven average sleep duration below recommended levels. Methods: We administered an anonymous 24-item survey to 412 students in grades 9–12, capturing weekday sleep duration, sleep quality, daytime fatigue, and cumulative GPA. Results: Only 21% of respondents met the 8-hour recommendation. Students sleeping fewer than 6 hours had a mean GPA 0.6 points lower than those sleeping 8 or more (p < 0.01) and were 3.1 times more likely to report daily fatigue. Conclusion: Short sleep duration is common in our student population and is associated with measurable academic differences, supporting later school start times and sleep-education programming.

Few habits shape a teenager's day as completely as the night before it. Yet between homework loads, extracurriculars, and the glow of a phone screen, sleep is often the first thing sacrificed. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommends 8 to 10 hours of sleep per night for adolescents, but national surveys suggest most fall well short. We set out to measure how short — and what it might be costing students academically.

Introduction

Chronic sleep restriction in adolescents has been linked to impaired attention, memory consolidation deficits, and mood disturbance in the broader literature. During puberty, a biological shift in circadian timing pushes sleep onset later, while school start times remain fixed early in the morning — a mismatch researchers have called “social jet lag.” Our study asked whether this mismatch is visible in our own student body, and whether it correlates with academic outcomes.

Methods

We designed a 24-item anonymous survey covering average weekday sleep duration, bedtime consistency, caffeine intake, screen use before bed, daytime fatigue, and self-reported cumulative GPA. The survey was approved by our faculty advisor and distributed to a stratified sample across all four grade levels. A total of 412 completed responses were collected (response rate: 68%). Respondents were grouped by sleep duration: under 6 hours, 6–7 hours, and 8 or more hours. Group means were compared using a two-sample t-test with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Only 21% of respondents reported sleeping 8 or more hours on school nights, while 34% reported fewer than 6 hours. Mean GPA declined steadily across sleep groups: 3.7 among students sleeping 8+ hours, 3.3 in the 6–7 hour group, and 3.1 among those under 6 hours — a difference that reached statistical significance between the shortest and longest sleepers (p < 0.01). Students in the under-6-hour group were 3.1 times more likely to report feeling fatigued “every day” or “most days.” Screen use within one hour of bedtime was reported by 78% of short sleepers, compared with 51% of students sleeping 8 or more hours.

Only one in five students in our survey met the recommended 8 hours of sleep on school nights. Study findings, 2026

Discussion

Our findings mirror national data and extend them with a local, school-specific picture. The association between short sleep and lower GPA does not prove causation — students under greater academic pressure may sacrifice sleep to study, creating a reverse-causality loop — but the strength and consistency of the relationship across grade levels is striking. The high prevalence of pre-bed screen use among short sleepers suggests a modifiable behavior worth targeting in student wellness programming.

Limitations

This study is cross-sectional and relies on self-reported sleep and GPA, both of which may be subject to recall bias. We could not control for underlying conditions such as anxiety or sleep disorders. A longitudinal follow-up using wearable sleep tracking is planned for the coming school year.

Conclusion

Sleep deprivation is the norm, not the exception, in our student population, and it is associated with meaningful differences in academic performance and daily fatigue. We recommend that the school consider later start times, coordinate major assignment deadlines across departments, and incorporate sleep education into the health curriculum. Students, meanwhile, may find the simplest intervention is also the most powerful: putting the phone down an hour earlier.
